Adversarial Robustness in Foundation Models: A Comprehensive Survey

RZ
Dr. Rachel Zhang, Dr. Thomas Mueller, Prof. Kenji Watanabe
Accepted at ICML 2026 · Published February 15, 2026
Abstract: We present a comprehensive survey of adversarial robustness in foundation models, covering both attack methodologies and defense mechanisms across language, vision, and multimodal architectures. Our analysis spans 847 papers published between 2022-2026, identifying key trends, open problems, and promising research directions.

1. Introduction

Foundation models — large-scale neural networks pre-trained on broad data and adapted to specific tasks — have become the dominant paradigm in modern AI. Models like GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, and LLaMA power applications ranging from chatbots and coding assistants to autonomous agents and scientific research tools. However, these models exhibit significant vulnerabilities to adversarial attacks, raising critical concerns about their deployment in high-stakes applications.

Adversarial robustness in the context of foundation models encompasses a broad spectrum of threats: adversarial examples that cause misclassification, prompt injection attacks that override model instructions, jailbreaks that bypass safety guardrails, data poisoning during training, and model extraction through carefully crafted queries. The attack surface has expanded significantly as models have become multimodal and agentic — capable of processing diverse inputs and taking actions in the real world.

2. Taxonomy of Attacks

We organize adversarial attacks on foundation models into five primary categories:

2.1 Input Perturbation Attacks

These attacks modify input tokens to change model behavior while preserving semantic meaning for human observers. In the text domain, this includes character-level perturbations (typos, homoglyphs), word-level substitutions (synonyms, paraphrases), and sentence-level reformulations. In the vision domain, imperceptible pixel modifications continue to be effective, with recent work showing that even CLIP-based multimodal models are vulnerable to L-infinity perturbations as small as epsilon = 2/255.

2.2 Prompt Injection

Prompt injection attacks target the instruction-following behavior of language models. Direct prompt injection involves crafting adversarial user inputs that override system instructions. Indirect prompt injection — the focus of much recent concern — embeds adversarial instructions in data that the model processes, such as web pages, emails, or documents. Our survey identifies 23 distinct indirect prompt injection techniques, categorized by delivery method (visual concealment, metadata manipulation, semantic embedding, dynamic assembly) and payload type (data exfiltration, tool hijacking, persona manipulation).

2.3 Jailbreak Attacks

Jailbreaks aim to bypass safety alignment and content policies. Recent techniques include multi-turn jailbreaks that gradually shift the conversation context, "many-shot" attacks that provide numerous examples of the desired unsafe behavior, encoding-based attacks that represent harmful requests in Base64 or other formats, and multi-language attacks that exploit weaker safety training in low-resource languages.

2.4 Data Poisoning

Data poisoning attacks compromise models during training by injecting malicious examples into the training dataset. This is particularly concerning for models trained on web-scraped data, where adversaries can create or modify web pages that will be included in future training corpora. Sleeper agent attacks — where poisoned models behave normally until activated by a specific trigger — represent an especially insidious variant.

2.5 Model Extraction

Model extraction attacks aim to reconstruct a proprietary model's weights or decision boundaries through carefully designed queries. Recent work demonstrates that extraction attacks against API-gated models have become increasingly practical, with some approaches achieving 95%+ fidelity with fewer than 100,000 queries.

3. Defense Mechanisms

Defense mechanisms have evolved rapidly but continue to lag behind attacks in many areas. The most promising approaches include:

  • Input sanitization: Detecting and removing adversarial content before model processing. Effective for simple injection techniques but easily bypassed by sophisticated obfuscation.
  • Instruction hierarchy: Establishing clear priority levels between system prompts, user instructions, and external data. Promising in theory but difficult to implement robustly.
  • Output filtering: Post-processing model outputs to detect and remove content resulting from successful injections.
  • Adversarial training: Including adversarial examples during model fine-tuning to improve robustness. Shows significant improvements but introduces performance tradeoffs.
  • Multimodal grounding: Cross-referencing information across modalities to detect inconsistencies that may indicate adversarial manipulation.

4. Open Problems

Despite significant progress, several fundamental challenges remain. The generalization problem — training defenses that transfer to novel attack types — remains largely unsolved. The robustness-capability tradeoff, where increased safety measures reduce model utility, is a persistent tension. And the evaluation problem — how to systematically measure adversarial robustness in a way that predicts real-world vulnerability — lacks a consensus solution.

We propose a standardized evaluation framework consisting of 12 benchmark tasks, 5 attack difficulty levels, and 3 defense posture categories. We hope this framework will accelerate research by enabling direct comparison across studies.
