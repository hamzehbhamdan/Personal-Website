How Much Sleep Do You Really Need?

The National Sleep Foundation recommends that adults (18-64) aim for 7-9 hours of sleep per night, with 7 hours considered the minimum for optimal cognitive function. Older adults (65+) may function well on 7-8 hours. While individual variation exists, consistent sleep deprivation — defined as regularly getting less than 6 hours — is associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, obesity, and depression.

Sleep debt is cumulative: missing an hour of sleep each night over a week creates a deficit equivalent to one full night of missed sleep, and this cannot be fully "repaid" in a single weekend recovery session. Chronic sleep restriction impairs cognitive performance in ways that individuals often cannot self-assess, creating a "performance gap" where people feel adequately rested but are measurably impaired.

Common Sleep Disorders

Insomnia

Insomnia affects approximately 30% of adults at some point in their lives and is characterized by difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking too early, combined with daytime impairment. Chronic insomnia is defined as occurring at least three nights per week for three months or more. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) is considered the first-line treatment, with demonstrated efficacy superior to sleep medications for long-term outcomes.

Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA)

OSA is characterized by repeated episodes of partial or complete upper airway obstruction during sleep, resulting in oxygen desaturation and fragmented sleep architecture. It affects an estimated 26% of adults aged 30-70, with significant underdiagnosis due to lack of awareness. Risk factors include obesity, male sex, older age, anatomical features, and family history.

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy remains the gold standard treatment for moderate-to-severe OSA. Adherence challenges are common but manageable with proper education, mask fitting, and follow-up support. For mild OSA or CPAP-intolerant patients, oral appliance therapy and positional therapy are evidence-based alternatives.

Restless Legs Syndrome (RLS)

RLS is characterized by uncomfortable sensations in the legs, typically described as creeping, crawling, tingling, or aching, that create an irresistible urge to move. Symptoms are most pronounced during periods of rest and in the evening, often severely disrupting sleep onset. RLS affects 7-10% of the population, with familial forms often appearing in younger patients and secondary RLS associated with iron deficiency, pregnancy, and end-stage renal disease.

Sleep Hygiene Principles

Evidence-based sleep hygiene recommendations include:

  • Maintain a consistent sleep and wake schedule, even on weekends, to reinforce your circadian rhythm
  • Create a bedroom environment that is cool (65-68°F), dark, and quiet
  • Avoid caffeine after 2 PM, as caffeine has a half-life of approximately 5-6 hours and can significantly disrupt sleep architecture
  • Limit alcohol consumption, particularly within 3 hours of bedtime. While alcohol may help you fall asleep faster, it suppresses REM sleep and causes fragmented sleep in the second half of the night
  • Avoid screens (phones, tablets, laptops, television) for at least 60 minutes before bed due to blue light suppression of melatonin production
